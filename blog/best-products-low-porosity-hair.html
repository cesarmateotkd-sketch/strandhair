Best Products for Low Porosity Hair: What the Lab Research Actually Shows

October 2026 · 10 min read

Conditioner that sits on top of your hair. Leave-in that turns white. Hair that takes forever to get wet and forever to dry, and feels coated by day two. If that sounds familiar, someone has probably told you that you have low porosity hair and to “use lightweight products.” That advice isn’t wrong, but it skips the part that actually helps: what gets into a hair fiber, what stays on the surface, and what changes that.

Lab researchers have studied exactly that, and the findings are more specific than most product lists suggest: which oil penetrates and which doesn’t, what heat does, and why the order of your products matters. What “Low Porosity” Actually Means

Every strand is wrapped in a cuticle: several layers of flat, overlapping scales, like shingles on a roof. On low porosity hair those scales lie flat and tight. That is a sign of an intact, healthy cuticle, not damage, and it is why the hair often looks shiny. It also means water and products have a hard time getting in, so they bead up, sit on the surface, and build up.

One thing to know before you buy anything: porosity is not something you can measure accurately at home. Researchers quantify it with lab methods such as gas sorption, which measures the actual pore volume inside a fiber (J Cosmet Sci, 2008). The popular glass-of-water float test mostly measures oils, buildup, and surface tension. Our porosity test guide covers the home tests and their limits.

The most useful home sign

Watch how your hair behaves on wash day. If water beads on it before it soaks in, it takes a long time to get fully wet, and it takes hours to dry, treat it as low porosity, whatever a glass of water says.

The Routine These Picks Are Built Around

  • Clarify every 2–4 weeks. Buildup blocks everything else, and low porosity hair builds up fastest.
  • Pre-poo with coconut oil. The protein-loss finding was for pre-wash use, so apply it before shampoo and wash it out.
  • Deep condition with heat. 15–30 minutes under a heat cap, steamer, or hooded dryer, over a plastic cap.
  • Leave-in on soaking-wet hair. Water first, while the hair is still dripping.
  • Light cream, then oil last, if at all. That is the LCO order; our LOC method guide explains both.

Step 2 · Pre-poo

Viva Naturals Organic Extra Virgin Coconut Oil

Best for: a pre-poo, applied 20–30 minutes before you shampoo View on Amazon →

This is the pick with the strongest evidence in this guide. In lab studies, coconut oil penetrated the hair shaft while mineral oil stayed on the surface (J Cosmet Sci, 2001), and as a pre-wash treatment it was the only one of three oils tested that reduced protein loss from both undamaged and damaged hair (J Cosmet Sci, 2003). The researchers credit lauric acid, its main fatty acid, which is small and straight-chained enough to get into the fiber; bulkier oils mostly stay on the surface.

Use it the way the studies did: on dry or damp hair before shampoo, then wash it out, so it never sits on low porosity hair as a coating. Some people with coarse hair find coconut oil leaves hair stiff; as a rinse-out pre-poo, that rarely shows up. Any plain virgin coconut oil works; this one is our standard oil pick.

Frequently Asked Questions

What products are best for low porosity hair? +

Light, water-based products used in the right order: a clarifying shampoo every 2–4 weeks, a deep conditioner applied with heat, a thin leave-in on soaking-wet hair, and at most a light cream and a little oil on top. Heavy butters and thick oils tend to sit on the surface and build up.

Is coconut oil good for low porosity hair? +

As a pre-wash treatment, yes, and it has the best evidence of any oil: lab studies found coconut oil penetrates the hair shaft and reduces protein loss when used before shampoo, while mineral and sunflower oil do not. Rinse it out rather than leaving it on as a sealant, since some people find it leaves coarse hair stiff.

Does low porosity hair need heat to deep condition? +

It helps more than any product swap. Lab studies show heat improves how much oil gets into hair and that ingredients diffuse into hair faster at higher temperatures. A heat cap, steamer, or hooded dryer for 15–30 minutes over a plastic cap is enough; avoid high direct heat.

Should I use the LOC or LCO method for low porosity hair? +

Most low porosity hair does better with LCO (liquid, cream, oil), because oil films reduce how much moisture hair takes in, so oil works best last. The key is water first and oil last; many people with low porosity hair skip the oil entirely.

Is the float test accurate for low porosity hair? +

No. Whether a strand floats depends on oils, buildup, and surface tension, not just porosity. Researchers measure porosity with lab methods like gas sorption. A more useful home sign is behavior: hair that takes a long time to get wet and a long time to dry, and products that sit on top.

Does low porosity hair need protein? +

No study tests protein needs by porosity. Low porosity hair usually has an intact cuticle, so it often needs less frequent protein, and some people find protein-heavy products leave it stiff. Watch your hair: stiff and straw-like suggests too much protein; mushy and over-stretchy suggests too little.
