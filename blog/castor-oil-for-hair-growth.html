Castor Oil for Hair Growth: What the Science Actually Says

May 2026 · 10 min read

CBy César, founder of Strand · studies linked in the text

Does castor oil grow hair? There’s no good evidence that it does. No study has tested castor oil on its own for hair growth in people; a 2026 dermatology review called its proposed growth mechanism unproven in practice (Cureus 2026). What castor oil does well is seal: it’s thick enough to coat dry ends and add slip. Below: what’s known, what isn’t, a rare but serious risk, and how to use it.

How fast hair grows is set by your follicles, and it varies: studies measured about 0.3 inches a month in African hair and about 0.47 in Caucasian hair (Br J Dermatol 2001). No oil has been shown to speed that up. What a sealing oil can do is protect ends from breaking, which helps you keep the length you grow.

What Is Castor Oil?

Castor oil is a vegetable oil pressed from the seeds of the Ricinus communis plant, native to tropical Africa and Asia. Its defining chemical characteristic is a very high concentration of ricinoleic acid — a hydroxy fatty acid that makes up roughly 85–95% of castor oil by composition. No other common plant oil has this fatty acid profile; it is what makes castor oil behave differently from coconut, jojoba, or argan oil.

Ricinoleic acid has several properties relevant to hair and scalp health:

  • Anti-inflammatory in lab work: ricinoleic acid reduced inflammation markers in animal and lab studies. Whether that helps your scalp hasn’t been tested, and dermatologists have reported that hair oils may worsen seborrheic dermatitis in Black patients (Skin Appendage Disord 2023).
  • Antimicrobial claims: often repeated online; we couldn’t find good evidence for an effect on dandruff, so treat it as unproven.
  • Very thick: castor oil is hundreds of times more viscous than water, which is why it works as a heavy sealant on ends, and why it can be hard to wash out.
  • “Humectant” claims: not shown for hair. Think of castor oil as a sealant, not a moisturizer: put it on after a water-based leave-in.

Does Castor Oil Actually Grow Hair? The Honest Answer

There are no large-scale, peer-reviewed clinical trials demonstrating that castor oil directly stimulates hair follicle activity or accelerates hair growth in humans. The evidence base consists primarily of:

  • Lab studies on ricinoleic acid and prostaglandins, the compounds involved in the hair cycle; a 2026 review says how this translates to real hair loss is uncertain (Cureus 2026)
  • Anecdotal reports from the natural hair community, which are real but usually come with other routine changes at the same time
  • One company-run trial of a rosemary + castor oil blend reported better results than coconut oil (Cureus 2025), but because it contained rosemary oil it can’t show what castor oil itself does
The rosemary oil trial often quoted next to castor oil (Panahi et al., 2015) studied rosemary oil, not castor oil, in adults with pattern hair loss. Castor oil has not been tested on its own in a controlled human trial for hair growth.

What castor oil can realistically do:

  1. Seals ends: a thick coat on the oldest, driest part of the hair, applied after a water-based leave-in
  2. Adds slip for detangling, and combing damage adds up stroke by stroke (Int J Cosmet Sci 2021)
  3. Doesn’t repair split ends: no oil does; trimming is the only fix
  4. Scalp massage is separate from the oil: the massage study usually quoted followed 9 men with no comparison group and measured thickness, not growth (Eplasty 2016)

The realistic benefit is retained length: if the ends break off as fast as the roots grow, hair looks stuck. A sealant plus gentle detangling helps tip that balance; it doesn’t make hair grow faster.

Jamaican Black Castor Oil vs Regular Castor Oil

This is the most common question in the castor oil conversation, and the honest answer is that the difference is smaller than marketing suggests.

Both types contain essentially the same active compound (ricinoleic acid) at similar concentrations. JBCO’s higher pH from ash content is sometimes cited as beneficial for scalp health, but no clinical study has confirmed this advantage over cold-pressed castor oil. The preference for JBCO in the 4C natural hair community is driven primarily by tradition and community consensus, which is a legitimate reason — just not a scientifically confirmed one.

Watch for fakes. True JBCO should have a thick, dark consistency and a smoky scent. Many products labelled “Jamaican Black Castor Oil” are diluted with lighter oils or dyed. Tropic Isle Living and Sunny Isle are the most consistently recommended authentic JBCO brands in the natural hair community.

Rare but serious: hair felting. Doctors have reported hair suddenly matting into a solid, un-detangleable mass right after castor oil was used, in one case together with coconut oil on long hair after washing; felted hair usually has to be cut off (Int J Trichology 2017). It’s rare, but to lower the risk: use small amounts, keep long hair in sections, and don’t pile or rub oiled hair on top of your head.

How to Use Castor Oil (Four Methods)

01

Scalp Massage for Follicle Health

Mix 1 teaspoon of castor oil with 2 teaspoons of a lighter carrier oil (jojoba, coconut, or argan). The lighter oil thins the castor oil enough to distribute it on the scalp without buildup and makes it significantly easier to wash out. Part the hair in sections and apply the mixture directly to the scalp using a dropper bottle or fingertip. Massage with fingertip pads (not nails) in small circular motions for 5–10 minutes. The massage is as important as the oil — it mechanically stimulates blood flow to the follicle. Do once or twice per week. Wash out with shampoo on your next wash day.

02

Ends Sealant in the LOC Method

Castor oil works well as either the O (oil) step or blended into the C (cream) step in the LOC method for high-porosity, coily hair. Apply a small amount (2–4 drops) to ends after your leave-in conditioner. For the LOC oil step, castor oil’s high viscosity makes it better suited to high-porosity or very dry hair than fine or low-porosity hair, where lighter oils are preferred. Do not apply castor oil to fine or wavy hair without significant dilution — the weight can deflate curl pattern and make washing out difficult.

03

Baggy Method Sealant for Ends

The baggy method uses castor oil as the final seal on ends before covering with a plastic cap overnight. Apply a generous amount of castor oil (or a castor-heavy butter blend) to ends, cover with a plastic bag or satin bonnet, and leave overnight. The occlusive seal of both the oil and the plastic traps moisture against the strand for 6–8 hours. Castor oil’s particularly high viscosity makes it one of the best oils for this application specifically — lighter oils evaporate through the bag; castor oil stays put.

04

Hot Oil Pre-Poo Treatment

Castor oil adds slip to a pre-poo, but the protection before washing comes from coconut oil, the oil shown to reduce protein loss (J Cosmet Sci 2003). Use castor as a small part of a coconut oil base. Mix castor oil with coconut oil in a 1:2 ratio (castor : coconut), warm gently in a bowl of hot water for 2 minutes, apply to dry hair from scalp to ends, cover with a plastic cap, and leave for 30 minutes to an hour. The coconut oil penetrates the cortex while the castor oil seals the surface. Shampoo out thoroughly on wash day.

How Much to Use: The Biggest Mistake

The most common castor oil error is using too much. Because castor oil is so viscous, excess application leaves hair feeling heavy, greasy, and difficult to wash out. It can also build up on the scalp, which some people find irritating.

General amounts by use case:
Scalp massage: 1 tsp castor oil + 2 tsp carrier oil, total per session
Ends sealant (LOC method): 2–4 drops, applied only to ends
Baggy method: enough to coat ends (roughly 1 tsp for long hair, less for shorter)
Hot oil treatment: 1 tbsp castor oil mixed with 2 tbsp coconut oil, total

Common Mistakes

  • Applying undiluted to the scalp: Pure castor oil is thick and hard to wash out; diluting it with a lighter oil makes it easier to spread and remove.
  • Expecting growth acceleration: Castor oil does not change how fast your hair grows. Measure stretched length every few months; progress shows up as retained length at the ends, not faster growth. The benefit shows up in retained length at the ends.
  • Using too much on fine or low-porosity hair: Even a few drops too many will make fine or low-porosity hair feel heavy and coated for days. Start with 1–2 drops and build only if needed.
  • Not clarifying: Castor oil must be clarified out of the scalp with a sulfate shampoo monthly. Using co-wash or sulfate-free shampoo exclusively while using castor oil regularly allows buildup that counteracts the scalp benefits.
  • Skipping the massage: Scalp massage is a separate habit from the oil. The study usually quoted (Koyama et al., 2016) followed 9 men with no comparison group and found slightly thicker hair, not faster growth.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does castor oil actually grow hair?

There’s no good evidence that it does. No controlled trial has tested castor oil alone for hair growth in people, and a 2026 dermatology review called its proposed mechanism unproven in practice. Castor oil is a useful sealant for dry ends, which can help you keep the length you grow.

What is the difference between JBCO and regular castor oil?

Jamaican Black Castor Oil is made from roasted castor beans; regular castor oil is cold-pressed without heat. Both contain roughly 85–95% ricinoleic acid. JBCO has a darker colour, smoky scent, and slightly higher pH due to ash from roasting. No clinical study has confirmed JBCO outperforms regular castor oil for hair growth. The preference for JBCO in the 4C natural hair community is long-standing and community-validated, but not clinically confirmed.

How often should I apply castor oil to my scalp?

No study sets a frequency. Once or twice a week, diluted with a lighter oil, is a common practice, and it’s easier to wash out that way. Skip scalp oiling if your scalp is flaky and inflamed: dermatologists have reported that hair oils may worsen seborrheic dermatitis in Black patients.

Can I use castor oil on my hair ends?

Yes, it’s one of its best uses. Put 2–4 drops on ends after a water-based leave-in, since an oil film keeps moisture in but doesn’t add it. Use small amounts; heavy amounts are hard to wash out.

Can castor oil cause hair loss?

Not in the usual sense, but there’s one rare, serious risk: doctors have reported sudden hair felting, where hair mats into a solid mass right after castor oil use and usually has to be cut off. Use small amounts, work in sections, and don’t pile oiled long hair on your head. Stop if your scalp gets irritated.

Is castor oil good for all hair types?

It works for all hair types, but the method and amount vary significantly. Fine and wavy hair (2A–2C) should use 1–2 drops on ends only, heavily diluted for scalp massage. Coily hair (3C–4C) tolerates and benefits most from full application — LOC oil step, baggy method, and scalp massage. Low-porosity hair of any type should use castor oil sparingly and only on ends, as its heavy weight can build up on the smooth, tightly-sealed cuticle.
