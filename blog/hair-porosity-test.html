Hair Porosity Test — How to Know Your Hair’s Porosity at Home

April 2026 · Updated November 2026 · 10 min read

CBy César, founder of Strand · studies linked in the text

How do you test your hair’s porosity at home? No home test has been validated against lab measurements, the float test included, so treat each one as a rough clue. The more useful signs are how your clean hair behaves: how fast it gets wet, how long it takes to dry, and whether products sink in or sit on top. Lab studies show porosity mostly tracks damage: bleaching nearly tripled the hair surface water can reach (J Cosmet Sci 2008). Below are the three common tests, what each can and can’t tell you, and what the research shows.

Why it matters: Porosity helps explain why the same product sinks into one person’s hair and sits on top of another’s. It isn’t the only factor — strand thickness, buildup and damage all play a part — but it’s a useful starting point for choosing products and the order you apply them in.

What Is Hair Porosity?

Hair porosity refers to your hair’s ability to absorb and retain moisture. It is determined by the condition of your hair’s cuticle layer — the overlapping, scale-like cells that wrap around the outside of every strand, like shingles on a roof.

When cuticles lie flat and tight, water and products have a harder time getting in (low porosity). When cuticles are raised, lifted, or have gaps between them, water enters easily but escapes just as fast (high porosity). The middle ground is medium or normal porosity. You’ll often read that warm water or heat “opens” the cuticle like shingles lifting; that picture hasn’t been shown directly (see the research table).

Porosity mostly reflects how much damage hair has taken. In lab tests, bleaching nearly tripled the hair surface that water can reach (J Cosmet Sci 2008). Hair that has never been colored or heat-styled is usually low to normal porosity; color, relaxers, heat and rough handling push it higher. No study has measured porosity by curl type, so 3C or 4C hair can be low, normal or high. That’s why virgin hair and color-treated hair can behave very differently even on the same head.

How to Test Your Hair Porosity at Home

Three tests are commonly used. None of them has been validated against lab measurement, so treat each as a rough clue rather than an answer. Test clean hair only (product buildup skews every result), and trust what you notice over a few wash days more than any single test.

Test 1 — The Float Test

Take a clean strand of shed hair and drop it into a glass of room-temperature water. Watch it for 2–4 minutes.

  • Floats on top: suggests low porosity — water isn’t penetrating the cuticle
  • Sinks slowly to the middle: suggests medium porosity
  • Sinks to the bottom quickly: suggests high porosity — water absorbed rapidly
Caveat: The float test is the most popular and the least reliable. Product buildup, scalp oil, strand thickness and where the strand came from all change whether it sinks, and no study has shown it tracks porosity. See the float test in our myths table.

Test 2 — The Spray Bottle Test

Lightly mist a small section of clean, dry hair with water from a spray bottle. Watch closely for the first 30 seconds.

  • Water beads and sits on top: low porosity — the cuticle is rejecting moisture
  • Water absorbs within 10–20 seconds: medium porosity — normal absorption
  • Hair immediately absorbs the water and looks dark: high porosity — rapid uptake

It’s quick and looks at a section of hair rather than one strand, but like the others it hasn’t been tested against lab measurements. Buildup can make water bead even on higher-porosity hair, so do it right after washing.

Test 3 — The Slide Test

Take a single strand of clean hair between your thumb and index finger. Slide your fingers from tip toward the root (against the direction of the cuticle scales).

  • Smooth, little friction: low porosity — cuticles are flat and tightly sealed
  • Slightly rough, some resistance: medium porosity — cuticles have some texture
  • Very rough, bumpy, catches fingers: high porosity — cuticles are raised or damaged

This test works best on clean hair at the mid-shaft (avoid the root where buildup concentrates, and avoid the ends which are always drier). It takes practice, and roughness can also come from buildup or split ends, so read it alongside the other signs rather than on its own.

Low Porosity Hair — What It Means for Your Routine

01

What low porosity hair needs

The challenge with low porosity hair is getting moisture in. Once it’s in, your hair holds it well — but that first step is a fight. Products tend to sit on the surface rather than absorbing, leading to white buildup on strands and hair that feels coated rather than moisturized.

Deep condition with warmth. In lab tests, heat increased how much oil hair took up, and conditioning ingredients diffused into hair faster as temperature rose (J Cosmet Sci 2005; Int J Cosmet Sci 2004). A thermal cap, hooded dryer or warm towel for 20–30 minutes is an easy way to use that. Treat warmth as a helpful nudge, not a switch that opens your hair.

Water first, light layers after. An oil film reduced how much moisture hair absorbed in a lab study (J Cosmet Sci 2007), so put a water-based leave-in on first and oil last. Heavy butters tend to sit on low porosity hair and build up, so many people do better with liquid leave-ins, aloe-based products and small amounts of light oil.

Clarify regularly. Buildup is the most common complaint with low porosity hair. A clarifying shampoo once or twice a month clears residue so your leave-in reaches the hair instead of last week’s product. (How often isn’t studied; go by feel.)

LOC or LCO: try both. No study compares the two orders. Many people with low porosity hair prefer LCO (Liquid, Cream, Oil); either way, water goes on first and oil after it, which is the part with lab support. See the LOC method guide.

High Porosity Hair — What It Means for Your Routine

02

What high porosity hair needs

The challenge with high porosity hair is keeping moisture in. Raised or gapped cuticles allow water to pour in and out freely, which is why high porosity hair can feel dry within hours of moisturizing, frizzes dramatically in humidity (absorbing atmospheric moisture through open cuticles), and tangles and breaks easily.

Seal after water. Raised cuticles let water leave quickly, so a sealing layer over your water-based leave-in helps hold it. No study compares LOC and LCO; many people with high porosity hair prefer LOC (Liquid, Oil, Cream).

Consider protein for damaged lengths. In lab tests, protein treatments reinforced damaged hair fibers (Polymers 2023). How often isn’t studied; a light protein product every few weeks is a common starting point, with heavier treatments for bleached or relaxed hair. Stop if hair turns stiff or brittle. More in our protein treatment guide.

Mind your pH. In lab tests, alkaline (high-pH) shampoos increased friction between hair fibers, which means more tangles (Int J Trichology 2014). Gentle, lower-pH cleansers and an acidic conditioner make sense for raised cuticles. A diluted apple cider vinegar rinse (1 part ACV to 3–4 parts water) is a common follow-up, though ACV rinses themselves haven’t been studied; once a week at most.

Richer creams can help. Many people with high porosity hair find thicker creams and butters hold moisture longer than light lotions. Apply them over a water-based leave-in, then seal with a heavier oil or shea butter if your ends still dry out fast.

Medium Porosity Hair — What It Means for Your Routine

03

What medium porosity hair needs

Medium porosity is usually the easiest to manage: water gets in without escaping too fast, so most leave-ins, creams and gels behave the way their labels promise.

The main goal for medium porosity hair is to keep it that way. Limit bleaching and color, use a heat protectant before thermal styling, and keep heat moderate. Medium porosity hair rarely needs regular protein unless you see damage; too much can leave it stiff.

What Causes High Porosity?

Most high porosity is acquired, and much of it is preventable:

  • Chemical processing — bleach, color, relaxers and perms break down the cuticle. In lab tests, bleaching nearly tripled the hair surface water can reach (J Cosmet Sci 2008), and that change stays in the processed length until it’s trimmed.
  • Heat damage — pores inside the hair rose rapidly between about 374 and 428°F (190–220°C) in lab tests (J Cosmet Sci 2011). Heat protectant cut combing breakage after flat-ironing (J Cosmet Sci 2011) but doesn’t make high heat safe. The damage stays in the affected length.
  • Mechanical stress — surface damage rose with every round of combing in lab tests on tightly coiled hair (see detangling in our myths table). Rough towel drying and detangling dry hair add to it.
  • Hard water — minerals in hard water can leave deposits that make hair feel coated, which can look like low porosity. Whether it damages hair over time hasn’t been well studied; a clarifying or chelating shampoo removes the deposits.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is hair porosity?

Hair porosity is your hair’s ability to absorb and retain moisture. It is determined by the state of your cuticle layer — the overlapping scales on the outside of each strand. Raised or damaged cuticles (high porosity) absorb water quickly but lose it just as fast. Flat, tightly packed cuticles (low porosity) resist water initially but hold it well once it penetrates.

Is the float test accurate for hair porosity?

No. No home porosity test has been validated, the float test included. Whether a strand sinks depends on product buildup, scalp oil and strand thickness, not just porosity. Use it as one rough clue alongside how your hair behaves: how fast it gets wet, how long it takes to dry, and whether products sink in or sit on top.

What are signs of high porosity hair?

High porosity hair absorbs water very quickly but dries out fast. Signs include: hair that gets wet within seconds of water contact, frizz in humid weather (open cuticles absorb atmospheric moisture), hair that feels dry within hours of moisturizing, tangles easily, and looks dull or lacks shine. Bleaching, relaxing, excessive heat styling, and aggressive brushing all cause or worsen high porosity.

What are signs of low porosity hair?

Low porosity hair resists water — it takes time to get fully wet and takes a long time to dry (sometimes 6–8 hours air dry). Signs include: products sitting on top of the hair rather than absorbing, white or grey buildup on strands after product use, and hair that feels coated or heavy rather than moisturized. No study has measured porosity by curl type, so 4C hair can be low, normal or high.

Can hair porosity change over time?

Yes. Bleach, color, relaxers, heat and rough handling raise porosity in the lengths they touch; in lab tests, bleaching nearly tripled the hair surface water can reach. New growth comes in without that damage. You can’t reverse it, but protein treatments, lower-pH products and sealing help damaged lengths hold moisture until they’re trimmed.

Should high porosity hair use LOC or LCO?

No study compares the two. Many people with high porosity hair prefer LOC (Liquid, Oil, Cream), and many with low porosity hair prefer LCO (Liquid, Cream, Oil). The part with lab support is the same in both: water-based product first, oil after it.
