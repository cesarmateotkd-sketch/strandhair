The LOC Method for Natural Hair: A Beginner's Complete Guide

Updated November 2026  ·  10 min read

CBy César, founder of Strand · studies linked in the text

The LOC method means layering a Liquid, then an Oil, then a Cream on freshly washed, soaking wet hair. Water is what moisturizes; the oil and cream slow it leaving. No study has tested LOC itself, but its core rule, water first and oil after, matches lab studies on how oil films affect water in hair. If your hair feels coated, try LCO (cream before oil).

If you have curly, coily, or wavy hair and you've ever felt like no matter how many products you use, your hair is still dry by day two — the LOC method is likely the missing piece in your routine.

The LOC method isn’t a product. It’s a sequencing technique: a specific order for layering products after washing. It has become one of the most widely practiced techniques in the natural hair community, particularly for Type 3 and Type 4 textures. No study has tested LOC itself, but one part of it has lab support: water first, oil after.

This guide explains exactly what the LOC method is, how to do it step by step, how to decide between LOC and LCO based on your hair's porosity, and which products work best at each layer.

What Does LOC Stand For?

LOC is an acronym for the three product categories applied in sequence:

  • L — Liquid (water or a water-based leave-in conditioner)
  • O — Oil (a penetrating or sealing oil)
  • C — Cream (a butter, cream moisturizer, or styling cream)

The logic: water is what moisturizes hair. Oil and cream don't add water; they slow it moving in and out. In a lab study, an oil film reduced how much water vapor hair absorbed (J Cosmet Sci 2007), which is why water goes on first and oil after it, never before.

Think of it like your skin: you apply a hydrating serum (liquid), then a facial oil to lock it in, then a moisturizing cream over the top. The same layering principle applies to your hair.

The Three Steps — In Detail

L
Step One

Liquid — Hydrate the Hair Shaft

The first step is applying a water-based product to freshly washed, still-wet hair. This is where actual moisture enters the hair. You can use plain water in a spray bottle, but most naturals prefer a water-based leave-in conditioner because it also adds slip for detangling and coats each strand with conditioning agents alongside the water.

Best products for the L step: Cantu Shea Butter Leave-In Conditioning Repair Cream, SheaMoisture JBCO Strengthen & Restore Leave-In, Mielle Organics Pomegranate & Honey Leave-In Conditioner.

Tip: Apply section by section on hair that is soaking wet, not just damp. The more saturated your hair is with water at this stage, the more moisture you lock in for the rest of the process.

O
Step Two

Oil — Seal the Cuticle

The oil step is where most beginners make mistakes. The oil in LOC is a sealant: a thin film over the water you just applied, slowing how fast it leaves. The oil is not the moisturizer.

Not all oils behave the same. In lab studies, coconut oil got into the hair shaft while mineral oil stayed on the surface (J Cosmet Sci 2001), and olive and sunflower oil also got in (J Cosmet Sci 2005). Castor, jojoba and avocado oil haven’t been tested this way. For the O step, any of them can form the film; heavier oils suit thick, dry ends and lighter ones suit fine hair.

Best oils for the O step: Jamaican black castor oil (heavy, for thick, dry ends), Mielle Rosemary Mint Oil (lightweight, better for 3A–4A), Cliganic 100% Pure Jojoba Oil (light).

C
Step Three

Cream — Lock, Define, and Finish

The cream layer serves two purposes: it adds a final sealing barrier over everything below it, and it provides curl definition, reducing frizz and encouraging clumping. Creams are typically butter-based or contain emollients like shea butter, mango butter, or coconut milk.

This is also the styling step — the cream is what shapes your wash-and-go, twist-out, or braid-out while locking in all the moisture from the first two layers.

Best creams for the C step: SheaMoisture Coconut & Hibiscus Curl Enhancing Smoothie (a long-time community favorite), Cantu Shea Butter Coconut Curling Cream (best value).

LOC vs. LCO — Which Should You Use?

You may have seen the term LCO — Liquid, Cream, Oil — which is simply the LOC method with the cream and oil steps swapped. This is not a mistake. For some hair types, LCO delivers better results than LOC. Here's how to choose:

Method Order Best For Why
LOC Liquid → Oil → Cream Hair that dries out fast (often high porosity: damaged or processed hair) Oil goes on right after water, then cream on top. A common choice for hair that loses moisture quickly. Not studied.
LCO Liquid → Cream → Oil Hair where products sit on top (often low porosity) Cream goes on before the oil, so the heavier film comes last. A common choice for hair that feels coated easily. Not studied.

How to tell your porosity: no at-home test, including the popular float test, has been validated. Go by how your hair behaves: does it dry out fast (often high porosity), or take a long time to get wet, with products sitting on top (often low porosity)? Porosity rises with damage such as bleaching (J Cosmet Sci 2008), and no study has linked it to curl type.

How Often Should You Do the LOC Method?

The LOC method is most effective on freshly washed, clean hair. That said, many naturals refresh between wash days by lightly spritzing with water and re-applying just the cream or oil layer to re-activate their style and add moisture back.

  • Type 4C/4B hair: Full LOC routine every wash day (every 7–10 days). Light re-moisturize mid-week with water + oil or cream.
  • Type 3B/3C hair: Full LOC routine every wash day (every 5–7 days). Refresh as needed.
  • Type 2C/3A hair: Consider LCO instead of LOC. Apply on wash day (every 3–5 days).

Common LOC Method Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using too little liquid: The L step must saturate your hair. If your hair isn't soaking wet when you begin, you're not sealing in enough moisture.
  • Using a heavy oil when you have low porosity hair: An oil film slows water getting into hair, so on low porosity hair keep the oil light and last. Many people find jojoba or argan easier than castor oil.
  • Applying products to dry hair: The LOC method only works on wet or very damp hair. The water is the moisturizer — everything else just locks it in.
  • Using too many products: Each layer should be applied thinly and evenly. More product doesn't mean more moisture — it usually means buildup and limp hair.
  • Skipping the oil: Many beginners go straight from leave-in to cream, but the oil layer is what slows water leaving; skipping it, or using LCO, is fine if your hair stays soft.

The Bottom Line

LOC itself has never been tested in a study, but its core rule, water first and oil after, matches what lab studies show about oil films and water. If you've been layering products without a system, adopting a consistent LOC or LCO routine will likely be the single biggest improvement you make to your hair's health and hydration.

The right products matter, but the sequence matters more. Start with your hair soaking wet, layer from lightest to heaviest, and be consistent. Give it two or three wash days before you judge it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the LOC method?

The LOC method is a hair moisturizing technique where you apply products in three layers: Liquid (a water-based leave-in conditioner), Oil (a penetrating or sealing oil), and Cream (a butter or thick cream). Each layer serves a different function to infuse, slow the loss of, and seal in moisture for natural hair.

What is the difference between LOC and LCO?

In LOC, oil goes on before cream; in LCO, cream goes before oil. No study has compared them. LOC is a common choice for hair that dries out fast, LCO for hair where products sit on top. Both put water first and oil after it, which is the part with lab support.

How often should you do the LOC method?

Most natural hair textures benefit from the full LOC method on wash day, then a lighter refresh every 2-3 days using just water and oil or a small amount of leave-in. Doing the full LOC method daily can cause product buildup — especially with heavy creams and butters.

What oil should I use for the LOC method?

In lab studies, coconut, olive and sunflower oil got into the hair shaft and mineral oil stayed on the surface. Jojoba, argan, almond, avocado and castor oil haven't been tested this way. For the O step any light layer works: lighter oils for fine hair, heavier ones such as castor for thick, dry ends.

Can you do the LOC method on dry hair?

It works best on freshly washed, soaking wet hair, because water is what moisturizes and an oil film slows water getting in. On dry hair, mist with water or use a water-based leave-in first, then oil and cream.

Why is my hair still dry after the LOC method?

Common causes: the liquid step has little water in it (water or aloe should be near the top of the ingredient list); oil went on before water; buildup is blocking products (clarify every 2 to 4 weeks); or the hair is damaged and loses water fast. Try LCO, lighter layers, or a deep conditioner with warmth.
