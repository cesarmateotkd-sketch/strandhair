Low Porosity Hair: What It Is and the Routine That Works

April 2026 · 11 min read

CBy César, founder of Strand · studies linked in the text

Low porosity hair is hair that takes in water slowly: it takes a long time to get wet, a long time to dry, and products tend to sit on top instead of soaking in. It is usually a sign of undamaged hair, because porosity rises with damage such as bleaching (J Cosmet Sci 2008). The fix is not more product. It is warmth while conditioning, water before oil, lighter products and regular clarifying.

If products sit on top of your hair instead of absorbing — you have low porosity. Water beads on the surface. Deep conditioner stays slippery instead of absorbing. Hair takes forever to get wet in the shower and forever to dry. These are not product failures; they are how low porosity hair behaves, and the routine below works with it.

How to Moisturize Low Porosity Hair

Water first, oil last, with warmth in between:

  1. Clarify every 2–4 weeks so buildup doesn’t block water.
  2. Deep condition with warmth (a thermal cap or steam, 15–30 minutes); heat increased how much got into hair in lab tests (J Cosmet Sci 2005).
  3. Apply a water-based leave-in to wet hair, section by section.
  4. Seal with a few drops of light oil last; an oil film on dry hair reduced how much water it took up (J Cosmet Sci 2007).

Choosing a conditioner? Pick a moisturizing one with glycerin, aloe or panthenol rather than a heavy protein treatment, and use it warm. For specific picks, see our low porosity hair products guide.

What Low Porosity Means (The Science)

Porosity describes how easily the hair shaft absorbs and retains moisture. Low porosity hair takes in water slowly. It is usually healthy, undamaged hair: porosity rises with damage, so hair that has never been bleached, colored or heat-styled tends to be lower porosity. You’ll often read that its cuticle scales “lie flat like closed roof shingles”; that picture is a simplification, and what matters in practice is that water and products take longer to get in.

Warmth helps, within limits. In lab studies, heat increased how much oil got into hair, and ingredients spread into hair faster at higher temperatures (J Cosmet Sci 2005; Int J Cosmet Sci 2004). The popular picture of heat “lifting the cuticle like roof shingles” has not been shown directly, so treat warmth as a helpful nudge, not a switch that opens your hair.

Signs You Have Low Porosity Hair

  • Hair takes a long time to fully saturate in the shower
  • Products sit on the surface rather than absorbing — hair feels coated, not moisturized
  • Product buildup occurs faster than expected
  • Hair takes a very long time to dry
  • Deep conditioner stays slippery on hair even after 30 minutes
  • Water beads on dry hair instead of absorbing immediately
  • Hair rarely feels truly dry — retains moisture well once it is in

No porosity test has been validated, so go by these signs. Our porosity testing guide explains the popular tests and their limits.

The Low Porosity Hair Care Routine

01

Clarify First — Every 2–4 Weeks

Buildup is a common problem for low porosity hair, because products tend to sit on top. Clarify with a chelating or sulfate shampoo every 2–4 weeks to strip this buildup completely. Between clarifying sessions, use a gentle sulfate-free shampoo. If you co-wash, still clarify regularly.

02

Apply Deep Conditioner to Warm, Damp Hair

After shampooing with warm water, do not rinse with cold water before deep conditioning. Apply your deep conditioner while hair is still warm from the shower. Warm hair takes up ingredients a little faster in lab tests, so this gives you a head start before you add heat. Work in sections to ensure full coverage.

03

Deep Condition Under Heat

Warmth is the step with the most lab support for low porosity hair. Use a hooded dryer, steam cap, or heat cap for a minimum of 20–30 minutes. In lab studies, heat increased how much got into hair; how much more conditioner gets in on real heads hasn’t been measured. Choose moisturizing deep conditioners with glycerin, aloe, and panthenol rather than heavy protein treatments.

04

Try LCO Instead of LOC

Many people with low porosity hair prefer the LCO (Liquid, Cream, Oil) order to LOC. Apply your water-based leave-in first while hair is still damp, then layer a lightweight curl cream, then seal with a light oil. The part with evidence is water before oil: in a lab study, an oil film reduced how much moisture hair absorbed (J Cosmet Sci 2007). Whether cream goes before or after oil hasn’t been tested; LCO is stylist practice that many low porosity readers find lighter. Choose the oil by feel: grapeseed, jojoba or argan seal without weight. Coconut oil is the oil with the best evidence for getting into hair, so it is a good pre-wash even if it feels heavy as a sealant.

05

Lightweight Products Only

Every product in a low porosity routine should be lightweight and water-based. Thick butters, heavy creams and rich oils tend to sit on the surface and build up. Check ingredient lists: humectants (glycerin, aloe vera, panthenol, honey) should appear in the first five ingredients of your leave-in and styling products. Avoid products with shea butter, cocoa butter, or lanolin as primary ingredients for regular use — reserve these for occasional sealing on the very ends only.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you moisturize low porosity hair?

Put water on first: a water-based leave-in on warm, wet hair. Deep condition under a thermal cap or steam for 15–20 minutes, since lab studies found heat increased how much got into hair. Seal with a few drops of light oil last; an oil film on dry hair reduces how much water it takes up. Clarify every 2–4 weeks so buildup doesn’t block the next wash.

What products are best for low porosity hair?

Lightweight, water-based products: a clarifying shampoo, a moisture-focused deep conditioner, and a light leave-in with humectants like glycerin or panthenol (panthenol has been shown to penetrate hair). Keep heavy butters for the ends. Coconut oil works as a pre-wash; light oils like grapeseed or jojoba work as sealants.

How often should you clarify low porosity hair?

Every 2–4 weeks, or sooner if hair looks dull, feels coated or takes longer than usual to dry. Low porosity hair shows buildup faster because products tend to sit on top. Deep condition right after clarifying. This schedule is stylist practice; no study has tested clarifying frequency.

Does low porosity hair need protein?

Usually not much. No study tests protein for low porosity hair, so watch your hair rather than following a rule: if it feels stiff or straw-like after a protein product, use less. If your hair is colored or heat-styled, its porosity is likely higher, and light protein may help.

What is the LCO method for low porosity hair?

LCO stands for Liquid, Cream, Oil: a water-based leave-in first, then a light cream, then a few drops of oil to seal. The water-before-oil part has lab support (an oil film reduced how much moisture hair absorbed). Whether cream before oil beats LOC hasn’t been tested; try both and keep the one that feels lighter.

Is low porosity hair the same as resistant hair?

Yes, both describe hair that takes in water and products slowly. Because porosity rises with damage like bleaching, low porosity hair is usually undamaged. The care is different, not harder: warmth, water before oil, lighter products and regular clarifying.
